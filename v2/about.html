The record

For a while, someone in my life kept asking why I wanted to study physics. Over time I understood that it wasn't curiosity. It was a test of whether I would talk myself out of it. His conclusion never changed: without a lifelong background in mathematics, and without a mind wired a particular way, I wasn't a serious candidate for this field. He said it gently, as a kindness, which somehow made it harder to hear.

These conversations came after I had been selected as Chief Scientist for NASA L'SPACE's Mission Concept Academy, where I led the science requirements for a proposed Mars subsurface exploration mission. They did not change his view. I didn't argue with him. I decided that the most honest answer to that kind of doubt is not a debate. It is a record.

Doubt from outside tells you very little about what is possible. Physics does not belong to one kind of mind or one kind of background.

Launch site

I'm from Pensacola, Florida, the oldest of three: two brothers and one sister. My family calls me the impulsive one, a reputation built largely on the one time I boarded a skydiving plane.

Before liftoff

I came to physics through a community college, a lot of self-study, and one NASA program that took my proposals seriously. Before that, I was figuring out what I wanted to be when I grew up. The answer turned out to be "someone who computes things about black holes."

Current trajectory

I'm a physics undergraduate studying the final moments of a black-hole merger. I compute quasinormal-mode predictions for LIGO–Virgo catalog events and document every input and assumption. I also work as a Project Coordinator at Parkhill, Smith & Cooper, and I bring the same care to instrument planning for lunar dust research.

Present coordinates

At 18 I moved to Austin, Texas, having sacrificed the beach for dry heat (I know, the audacity), and I now live in Dallas. My 3-year-old Rat Terrier, Tuxedo "Tux" Copeland, supervises all of it.
